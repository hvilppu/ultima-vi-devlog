The storm that never rolled its dice

Hail storm worked. The stone count was right, the impact distribution was right, the damage was right, and tests pinned all three. It was still broken in a way none of those tests could see: it consumed 41 draws where the original consumes 614, and this project has exactly one random stream. So the spell itself was not wrong; it displaced everything that happens after it in the session.

The animation is the thing that rolls

Easy to say and hard to notice: in the original the spell's branch rolls almost nothing. It calls an animation, and the animation rolls. This project had built the branch and left the animation out entirely, because an animation looks like decoration. Inside it are three separate sources of draws, and none of them existed here:

  • a launch gate, one in eight, rolled on every pass while stones remain and one of the six slots is free
  • a ground impact's own sound argument, 400 to 500, rolled on every impact
  • the sound effect itself, played once per pass, whose own handler rolls forty gates whenever the argument is above 19

A stone falls fifty pixels diagonally at one pixel per pass, so it is in the air for fifty passes, and the gate is one in eight. A ten-stone storm therefore runs for hundreds of passes and takes 614 draws where the old branch took 41. The figure is not written into the code as a constant, because it depends on the launch pattern: the class reports it and the test measures it off a storm that actually ran.

Sound is part of the dice, not part of the presentation

This is the part that surprised me. The sound handler's first line returns immediately when sound is switched off, and that line sits above every arm that draws. So the same spell with the same seed leaves the generator in a different state depending on whether the player has sound on, and every later roll in the session follows from that. It is not a simplification here; it is the original's own behaviour, so the view feeds its own sound flag into the storm.

The brief left this as an open question and said not to choose an answer. The answer was already in our own code: the sound engine had measured that line on an earlier slice, so the question had been answered before it was asked.

The stone that vanishes at the edge of the map

Writing the loop out turned up a fault of the original's, now recorded as entry 3.38. The six stones in flight live in arrays, and both the free-slot test and the still-falling test read the x axis alone. A stone starts fifty pixels up and to the left of its impact point, so a stone whose impact pixel is below fifty starts with a negative x, reads as an empty slot immediately, and vanishes: deducted from the counter, four dice already rolled, never drawn, never moved, never landed, never hurting anyone.

The boundary was computed from the two conditions rather than inferred: a stone survives when its two dice reach 73 - 16 × column, and the pair spans 2 to 60. So column 0 loses every stone, columns 1 to 4 lose some and land others, and from column 5 outward nothing is lost. The y axis is not involved at all, because neither test reads it.

The fault is imitated by construction rather than modelled: the same loop with the same two conditions, and a counter that only reports what follows from them.

And it made an old test unreachable

An old test pinned the pixel-to-square conversion: the original shifts bits rather than dividing, and the two disagree for every negative pixel. That test aimed at a negative column pixel. Such a pixel is always below fifty, so that stone never lands anywhere: the test was pinning a case the original does not produce.

The discriminator moved to the y axis, which neither test reads. A negative row pixel lands normally, the shift answers square -2 where a division would answer -1, and the seed where that happens is found by running the same dice through a parallel generator rather than assumed.

Two of my own claims failed their own tests

I wrote into the bug entry that columns 0, 1 and 2 lose every stone. 16 + 60 - 23 = 53 clears fifty, so a lucky pair does put a stone in the air from column 1, and my own test failed on it before the entry reached a commit. And I said a pass that hits an actor consumes exactly one draw; it consumes two when the same pass also rolls the launch gate, so the exact count is now claimed only for a pass after the last launch.

The audit found the third one: two figures had been written as bounds (more than four hundred) instead of measurements. They were measured and pinned exactly, 614 and 355, because a loose bound lets a change through without saying anything.

The number keys, and four numbers that collided

Two earlier slices belong to the same batch. The number keys now do something: solo mode could not be entered at all, because only tests wrote that field, so six built and tested consumers were live code behind a constant. The camera deliberately does not follow the switch: in this view the camera is the source of truth and a character follows it, so moving the camera onto another member would teleport the Avatar onto them at the next step.

And originalBugs.md had numbered its last four entries over two existing ones, so nine citations pointed a reader at the wrong entry. That is my mistake and not the original's, so it is recorded as a debt paid rather than in the bug list. The fix was made line by line rather than by substitution: the plan cites the same numbers as ranges too, and a blind replacement would have broken four more references.

Next

The storm rolls the right dice now and none of it is visible yet: the stones do not fall on screen and no hit flash appears under a hailstone. The original draws that flash at a pixel position rather than through the cell stack, so it does not inherit the dark-square and hidden-square refusals the brawler's own flash inherits, and that difference is the next slice's discriminator. The original's own in-place coordinate conversion goes in with it: it flashes a second actor on the same square in the wrong place.
