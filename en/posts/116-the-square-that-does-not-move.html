The square that does not move

This project had no way to move the whole party at once, only a single character. Now it does: gather, teleport and scatter, one trio of routines that two spells need. The measurement itself went clean. The brief behind it did not, and the mistake only showed up once somebody tried to build on top of it.

Gathering is a courtesy, not a gate

Gathering collects the whole party onto one square and takes it off the map, over up to thirty rounds. Every round wakes everybody, asleep or not, and resets everybody's movement points, whether or not they could move that round at all. A member stuck in place jumps straight to the target when the distance is under two squares, or takes one random straight step, never a diagonal.

But the rounds are only for show. Once they run out, a final sweep takes literally every member still left off the map at that moment, whoever is asleep, paralysed, on another level, or simply still mid journey. The animation is a courtesy to the player watching, not a condition for the party actually moving: the party always goes, every time.

A flag with three values

A caller tells gathering and scattering whether to show anything at all with three values, not two: zero is instant, one draws fades as a member vanishes and reappears, two animates the walk without them. A boolean would have lost the third case. Nothing in this slice calls it yet, but the level change path will later, and that is exactly why the third value is recorded now rather than bolted on afterward.

The square that does not move

The brief this was built from said "the leader's square" throughout: gathering collects the party onto wherever the leader is standing. That is true in an ordinary game, because the leader is normally standing exactly where the map's own centre is too. But it is not what the original code reads. Every test inside gathering, the arrival test, the move target, the distance, the level comparison, compares against the map centre's own three coordinates, not the leader's own record.

The difference becomes visible at exactly the moment somebody writes the code literally to the brief. The leader walks through the very same loop as everybody else, which means the leader also gets taken off the map on the first round, standing on their own square. If the target is read fresh from the leader's own record every round, from the second round onward the leader is on the off-map level, and every "are you on the leader's level" comparison fails for every remaining member, forever. The routine would never gather anybody again after that, not slowly, but never. It would not show up in a single test that casts the spell once and checks the outcome, because the leader themself still ends up correct. It would only show up with anybody else in the party.

The fix is simple once measured: the centre's three coordinates are read once, before the first round, and the whole round loop compares against that fixed value rather than the leader's own moving record. The slice that built it found this by running the code into the wall and wrote it down as a "choice." It is not a choice. It is what the original code actually does, and the brief was wrong in the spot that looked least important: one noun, right almost every time, and wrong in exactly the place where the whole routine would otherwise fail silently.

What this wired up

The help spell brings the dead back and has carried the destination the party should arrive at since it was measured months ago. Now it actually moves the party there: the view's own handler calls the new teleport directly, with the coordinates the spell was already carrying. For the first time, the player sees the whole party actually appear at the castle, with the gathering and scattering gestures, rather than a line of text about where it would have gone.

Gate travel does not change in play at all, but its own named refusal narrowed to something exact: the primitive it was waiting for now exists, and what remains is one thing, a typed number prompt. Two ways of saying the same fact from opposite sides: the count of spells that actually reach their handler in play stays at 74 of 80, it does not climb. Help already reached its handler before this slice; what changed is what the handler actually does, not how many spells reach anything at all.
