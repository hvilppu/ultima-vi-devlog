Notes from a 1:1 reimplementation in C# and Unity.

The gap in the wall

The sea started showing up, but a hole appeared beside every doorway. Some objects cover two squares, and I was drawing half of them.

Once the starting position moved off the open ocean and into Britain, a new fault became visible: the wall beside doorways had a hole in it. Terrain drew, furniture drew, but part of each door frame was missing.

Flags I had already decoded

The answer was in a file I had decoded earlier and then never used. Two bits in the display byte of TILEFLAG say nothing about how a tile looks and everything about how big it is:

BitMeaningObjects in the world
0x80two tiles wide799
0x40two tiles tall661
0xC0both, so two by two225

My renderer drew every object as a single 16×16 tile in its own square. For those 1685 objects that meant half of each one was never drawn. Door frames and tall wall sections happen to be exactly that set, which is why the fault appeared beside doors rather than scattered randomly.

Which way the missing half belongs

The tile number below a flagged tile has artwork of its own, so the pairing is obvious. The direction is not. Rather than guess, I asked the data.

The reasoning is simple. If the engine draws the second half into a neighbouring square, that square cannot hold an object of its own, because the content would appear twice. An empty neighbour should therefore be far more common for flagged objects than for ordinary ones:

CaseNeighbour empty
Wide object, square to the west94 %
Tall object, square to the north95 %
Baseline: ordinary objects, square to the west48 %

The flagged tile is the bottom right corner of the object. A wide one reaches one square west, a tall one reaches one square north.

Two by two, and how it was settled

For the 225 objects carrying both flags the thing covers four squares and needs four tiles. The tile numbers are consecutive, but in what arrangement? I implemented the most plausible convention, anchor at the bottom right with the preceding numbers filling west and then north, and labelled it honestly as a guess.

A guess is a poor place to leave something, so I measured it. A correctly assembled block is continuous: along its internal seams there are pixels on both sides. A wrongly assembled one breaks. I scored the seams of all 21 distinct two by two tiles under both arrangements: 

arrangement                      seam score   wins
west first (my implementation)          921    21 / 21
north first (the alternative)            80     0 / 21

An elevenfold difference, with no losses and no ties. That settles it. The advantage of a measure over eyeballing is that it covers all 21 cases rather than the four I had the patience to look at.

A second fault found alongside

Fixing this exposed another fault with the same root. The renderer only walked squares inside the window. A wide object standing one square east of the window still reaches into it, and was never drawn. The east and south edges of the view therefore had the same hole whenever something large stood just past the border.

The scan now extends one square beyond the edge and drawing is clipped to the window bounds. That has its own test: it places the view so a wide object falls just outside, and requires the window's edge column to change.

Tests

Five new ones, and two of them ask the right question following the lesson of the earlier mistake. They do not compare the renderer against itself, they compare against terrain: the square west of a wide object must differ from what bare terrain would give. If the overhang goes undrawn, they fail.

There is a guard as well: if the world contains no double-sized objects at all, the test fails. Otherwise the whole group could quietly become vacuous if the search condition ever breaks.
