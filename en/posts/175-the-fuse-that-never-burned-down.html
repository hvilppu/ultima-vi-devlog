The fuse that never burned down

The schedule is finished now: all eighteen behaviours have an arm of their own, or a measured reason not to need one. A guard finally walks its own post, a brawler picks a fight with whoever is nearest, and a townsperson told to converse walks up to you and opens a conversation unasked. A powder keg whose fuse was already lit finally burns all the way down: the blast reaches the kegs beside it and blows one door off every square it touches. And a flying creature now stops at another flying creature, where it used to slide right through. In between, this stretch spent a good part of its time correcting its own numbers, four of them my own brief's mistakes, and the build runs caught every one of the four.

A guard walks its own post

Twelve schedule entries name a guard's direction, and none of them did anything before this slice. The arm is built now, to four measured rules: exactly half the time the guard waits and spends five points, where every other arm uses one chance in eight; the direction comes from the schedule mode only when the guard is standing exactly on its scheduled square, and from its current facing otherwise, and that mode comparison is what tells a guard apart from a patroller; a blocked step turns through exactly 180 degrees and restores the failed attempt's points first, so it costs one move and not two; and the second attempt's result is never read at all, so a guard blocked both ways simply stays put.

The rate is measured and recorded on both sides in the comment: this seed's coin reads 0.4994, and the one-in-eight constant, run rather than predicted, reads 0.124175, well clear of the coin. Two other rate bands had already been redone earlier the same day for having been padded out to clear a theoretical figure instead of a measured one, and this is not one of those.

RestorePoints moved out of the combat AI to sit beside Spend, because a patrol is not combat, and its five existing callers came along with the count unchanged. The same move as the alligator constant that the mover brief pulled out of the slime spell earlier.

The brawl, the conversation, and a finished schedule

The last two scheduled behaviours are built, and after them all eighteen have an arm or a measured reason not to need one. The brawl is loitering with a punch in it: one turn in four it looks for an opponent within five squares of the brawl spot, not of itself, closes in if the opponent is far, and if one is already adjacent it lands a blow and walks back to its own spot. Everything else falls through to ordinary loitering. The opponent pick needed nothing new, because the target choice already skips the alignment filters for the same five modes the original skips them for, the brawl among them. The blow sets the victim's hurt-this-turn bit and deals no damage.

Conversing walks the NPC to the nearest visible party member and opens a conversation on arrival, and it shares its code with the thief mode. The mode is overwritten to loiter before the thief test runs, which is originalBugs.md 2.30 imitated: the entry arm is never branched on at all, because the original cannot tell the two modes apart once the overwrite has run. A fault test that skipped the overwrite for a thief, which is the forbidden working theft, fails its own assertion: the theft must never actually run, and it does not.

The build run corrected my brief by reading six lines further than I had. I wrote that the hit display is wholly gated by one flag. It is not: a sleeping victim wakes outside that gate, the wake also restores the original shape, and the flag resets itself at the end of the turn, so it is a one-shot suppressor rather than a setting. The wake is the visible half now that the postures exist: a brawler's punch gets a sleeping townsperson up.

Three near misses the run caught in its own tests are worth recording plainly. A rate test compared a second stream from the same random generator to itself instead of driving the actual code. A gate test put the actor and the opponent on the same square, so it could not tell the two cases apart. And a destination test put the opponent exactly on the target square, so swapping the two destinations the other way round would have been invisible. All three were found and fixed before the suite was trusted.

The fuse that never burned down

A lit powder keg burned and nothing happened. The detonation is built now and the chain is closed end to end rather than left as a rule nobody calls: the fuse now answers which keg is due, the world clock ticks it and skips it during a time stop the same way the original's own early return does, and the view detonates it and applies each hit through the same damage path the fireball's wind already uses.

The rule itself, as measured. A keg in a pack explodes at its carrier's own square, walking the holder chain until something is sitting on the map, which is the branch a build skips because a keg on the ground is the obvious case. The keg is deleted before the hit list is built, so it is never in its own list. One to sixty points of damage per hit, to single objects only, so a stack survives a blast that hurts the person standing beside it. And the chain reaction is five wide and four tall, because the column loop is inclusive and the row loop is not, so the square two rows below is never visited: a five-by-five build fails exactly that one test case and nothing else. The door cascade tries four types in a fixed order and blows the first acceptable one, proved on a square that holds a magically locked steel door and an ordinary oaken one at once and loses the oaken one.

The build run also did a structural cleanup that was already due, rather than pushing it further out: three explosion-scan helpers were local functions inside the spellbook cast, and the keg needed the same ones, so they became instance methods, and there is now one definition instead of two. The same kind of move this project's own order-of-work rule asks for while the file is still one file.

And it corrected my brief. I asked for "something in the running world that owns an instance of the fuse," and the view has held one since an earlier slice, and had already lit it. What was missing was only the clock's call and the detonation itself, which makes the defect sharper than I wrote it: the fuse was lit and simply never burned down. I counted an owner as absent without grepping for the field first.

The explosion's equivalence to the original's own routine was re-derived rather than taken on trust from a documented return value: sixteen trails on a fixed offset pattern, and both offset tables match the original's order exactly; the centre tile's own actors are appended under three conditions, all three present; the break on a candidate tile that blocks missiles, present through the same tile bit; and the documented terminator, absent from the returned content. And the damage skip condition the brief had left unmeasured is in the original's own comment: four is gold, where the whole amount is the count; two is a stack; zero is a single object, so the blast only ever damages the zero type, and a ready primitive elsewhere in the engine already answers exactly that, so no second table had to be written.

The fumarole's two arms are left for the next slice, both named rather than left as an uncalled coin again: wiring them needs reading the eruption flag's own lifecycle and working out where the end of turn sits in this project's own loop.

A creature stops another creature

A flying creature flew over terrain once the mover's own facts were wired in, and it still flew over other creatures. The actor half is built now: the collision entry gains its own wall bit and whether its class flies, and the flier's branch lets an object through on a non-wall tile and drops a non-flying actor into the same common tail, which stays unchanged. Being ethereal was checked rather than assumed: the original's own ethereal branch only ever exempts an object and never an actor, so the existing fallback already answered correctly and there was nothing to add.

Fourteen live winged gargoyles are the only flying class in the running world. Of the five small critter types, two fly, and neither is live in the owner's world, and the one live critter does not fly, so the tail already exempts it correctly today. The headline rule therefore does not meet a player today; it only becomes reachable through the creature generator and egg hatching. What does meet a player today is the half the previous slice built: an ordinary actor blocks a flier, because the gargoyles are live and every townsperson is an ordinary actor.

And a number I had been repeating all day is wrong. 198 of 200 that block a walker is the original's own count, whose tail blocks an actor outright regardless of its own tile. Measured here twice, once by the build run against the real character file through the production call and once by me independently: it is 187 for an ordinary mover and 184 for the Avatar. The arithmetic is 200 live, minus 12 with no usable own tile, minus the one live small critter, and for the Avatar minus the three able non-leader party members the swap exception exempts. Those twelve are the remaining divergence from the original, and they are ours: the adapter skips a character whose tile cannot be read rather than inventing a rule. 198 was a pure object-type classification that folded in neither exemption, and I repeated it across several commit messages and a later brief as though it described this project. All four numbers are now pinned by one test.

A smaller miscount of my own turned up in the same wiring work. My brief said the view has 41 of these call sites and 39 that must not change. It is 40 appearances, two of them inside comments, so the real count is 38 call sites and 36 that must not change. I had the grep output on screen and counted the lines by eye instead of running wc -l, which this project's own rules say to do first and say costs nothing, and I had not separated the comment hits from the code, the same distinction I had been applying to everyone else's claims all day.

What the save milestone's "done" mark does not say

M18 is marked done except for one parity test, and the mark is defensible on its own terms: the milestone's gate is a parity test rather than a player-facing feature, as its own first bullet says, and the tools side meets it. The loader builds a live world from the owner's own save files, and fourteen tests prove the writer against those same files, sixty nine files and every object exactly once.

What the mark does not show is that in the game, saving is wired and loading is not. And that entry went stale inside the same day, because I wrote in it that the loader sits in a Tools assembly Unity cannot see. Its readers do not: both are in the engine. I looked at the loader and drew a conclusion about its dependencies without searching for where they live, which is this very day's own recurring mistake. What is on the wrong side is only the format bridge, and the decision is made rather than open: the two writers move into the engine beside the parsers that read their output and write to memory, because new constructors would mean writing the same hundred and seventeen lines of field mapping a second time in the other direction, which is two copies of one piece of knowledge.

Found by chasing a symptom rather than by looking for it. U6DefaultCommand has a ClearForLoad with two test references and no production caller, and the view's own comment says it is waiting for a game load. It is waiting for a load that does not exist.
