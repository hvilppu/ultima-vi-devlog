Seven doors, not eight

The previous slice got casting to run every one of its gates, take the reagents and spend the mana and the movement points. Nothing happened after that: nothing ever connected the chosen spell number to any of the eight handlers this project had already built. This slice is that connection. And the moment it was built, it turned out there are seven handlers, not eight.

The map already existed, just unused

The dispatcher's own map was measured months ago: which of eight shared handlers resolves which spell, and which 19 spells share nothing with anyone. The map is not this slice's own work. This slice's work is the table that reads it and actually calls the right handler, given a spell number and a target-already-given flag, nothing more: no rules, no costs, no gates, because all of that already existed on the caller's side.

One name that never had a house

The map names eight handlers. Listing them out to build the connection, seven turned up a file, a class, tests, everything measured and present. The eighth turned up nothing. Not a partial version, not leftovers, nothing: the map knew it existed, but nobody had ever written a line for it. The seven spells the map points at that name are therefore their own named refusal rather than an eighth handler: no stand-in invented, because in this project a stand-in that looked harmless has three times already turned into a real fault the moment something actually called it.

Nineteen was right, eighty percent of the story was the wrong question

The plan and the map have always said 19 spells have their own branch, sharing no handler with anyone. That number is still right for its own question: how many spells never share anything with another. But this slice's own question is different: how many of the 80 numbers actually refuse today, when the dispatcher is called. The answer is 26, not 19, because the seven spells the map promises an eighth handler to never get one. Two different numbers for two different questions, and the plan's own number was not bent to match what building gave: building was right, and the plan's text got to say so.

A measuring tool that counted right and wrong at the same time

The map's own measuring script always said "80 cases", and that was true as a sum but wrong in content. One spell was missing entirely from the dispatcher's own scanned text, because the range ended four lines too early. Right next to it, in the same switch block, sits one case a player can never cast: it is only the monsters' and wands' own call point, not one of the eight circles of ten spells. That case happened to fill the exact slot the missing spell left empty, so the sum looked whole from two faults that cancelled in the count but not in the content. The two faults added up to a right answer while each half was wrong: exactly the situation where a single integer hides the thing that needed looking at.
