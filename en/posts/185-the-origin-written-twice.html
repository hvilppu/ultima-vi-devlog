The origin written twice

The gem's peer map needed one number it did not have: the origin of the forty by forty area the game keeps loaded around the party. I measured it, and then I went looking for it in this project. It was already here twice, and the two copies were not the same function. The difference falls on exactly the values the peer map's own edge clamps are keyed to, so with the wrong reading two of those four clamps are unreachable code.

Two maskings, sixteen coordinates

The original computes AreaX = (MapX - 0x10) & 0x3f8. In this project the egg sweep computed (c - 16) & ~7 and the path window computed it with the original's own mask. Both round down to a multiple of eight. The difference is that 0x3f8 also drops everything above the world's own ten bits: 

coordinate     16 or more     below 16
the original   agree          1008 (0-7), 1016 (8-15)
the egg copy   agree          -16, -8

distinct origins in all                   128
coordinates where the two differ           16

Sixteen coordinates in the world's north western corner. That is easy to read as harmless, and it would have stayed harmless, if the peer map had not happened to be the one place where the number decides something.

The discriminator is the map's own clamp chain

On the surface the peer map clamps its picture with four conditions, and two of them compare the origin against 0x3F0 and 0x3F8. Neither is reachable without the mask wrapping: no coordinate inside the world produces them by stepping sixteen back. I ran all 1024 coordinates through both readings and listed the reachable origins, and the other two clamps (0x3E0 and 0x3E8) are reachable either way, so they are not evidence about either. Half the chain settles the question and half does not, and the test says which half out loud.

Beside it is the measurement I liked most. The view's own offset inside the area cannot tell the two maskings apart at all. It is (c - AreaX - 5) & 0x1f, and the two origins differ by 1024, which is a multiple of 32, so the mask swallows the difference on every one of the 1024 coordinates. That is precisely why the wrong origin survived: the most natural check, "does the view land in the right place", could not have failed.

The loop gave me five things my measurement did not

The previous round measured peer_tile, which is the decision for one square: five colours and two chequer phases. That was not a picture. Reading the drawing loop itself produced five things the class did not have:

  • the whole picture is filled black before anything is drawn
  • a multi-tile object also marks the squares it reaches back over, with tile numbers -1 west, -2 north and -3 north west
  • an actor is told from an object by its number, objNum < 0x100
  • the first party member is skipped unless the player controls it
  • and the object pass does not respect the edge clamps the terrain pass does

That last one does not look like a fault read from either direction. The clamps are computed once, the terrain loops use them, and the object walk searches the whole area. So an object standing in a clamped strip draws its mark onto the black ground with no terrain under it, and this project now does the same.

The border covers the picture's outermost two squares

The picture is 160 pixels, forty squares of four, drawn into the region 8 to 167. The map window's own border is then redrawn into the same rectangle. The border is eight pixels wide and a square is four, so the routine computes and draws 1600 squares and the player sees 1296 of them. The arithmetic closes from two directions: 167 - 8 + 1 = 160 = 40 × 4, and 8 + 160 + 8 = 176, which is the map window's width. The picture is not fitted inside the frame but under it, and the outer edge is work nobody sees.

Thirteen rows that said "not built"

The use command's document carries a table whose status column said not built for thirteen rows at once: the cannon, the crystal ball, the fountain, the gem, the orb of the moons, the digging tool, the potion, the powder keg, the sextant, the silver horn, the staff, the telescope and the torch. All thirteen are in the engine and wired into the view. I measured that by running every type through the dispatch and counting the arms, not by reading the table.

And it was not harmless. The duration spells class quoted that very row as evidence that the gem command is not built, and the same row was still standing in a test's expectation. An absence claim in a document rots the same way one in a comment does, and a status column is the most read of them all.

One new trap went into the environment document: Does.Not.Contain(int) resolves to the string overload in Unity's own NUnit, so four lines that compiled here did not compile there. It turned up after 5653 tests were already green, which is the separate compile check doing its job for the fifth time.

Next

The peer map draws now, and the owner can reach it with a gem or with an Inspector tick that is the original's own debug key rather than a shortcut invented here. One thing is still owed: the visibility fill, without which a cave shows whole. The surface never runs the fill at all, so the picture above ground is finished. And the screenshots of the hail storm, the telescope, the cannon and now the peer map are all still waiting.
