Fifty named messages

The previous round exported 893 string literals into a runtime file and classified each one. This round was supposed to be the one that fills in the message index seven earlier slices had left unset, a short, mechanical piece of filling in. Instead it found that its own brief was wrong three times over, and that one earlier measurement, however careful and discriminated it was, had not been the whole truth.

A module the first measurement could not see

The first round counted 893 string literals the decompilation gives an offset, and measured a base for each per source module. It was a careful measurement: the right base landed 880 of 893, and the wrong ones landed almost nothing. It still was not the whole population. msgs.c carries its own data segment, and its offsets are recorded in a different form than everywhere else, so the first round's own enumeration never saw it at all. The segment holds 50 named messages, and not one of them was among the 893.

One base for the whole segment lands 50 of 50. Moved one byte forward it lands 0, one byte back 0, sixteen bytes forward 0, sixteen bytes back 1. A gap like that between the right reading and the wrong ones leaves nothing to argue about. These 50 are also the only messages in the whole program that have a name, which is exactly the identity a waiting message index needs.

Seven fields that counted slices, not fields

The brief called this round "filling in the seven waiting message index fields". Seven earlier slices had each said they were leaving one index unset, and the figure was copied from slice to slice without anyone counting it again. Between them they actually left twelve places waiting, on eleven distinct lines, because telekinesis and wind change's own direction prompts turned out to be the very same line, and one slice left three open on its own. Two of those twelve are also named globals, not inline literals, the opposite of what their own field documentation said. Four of this project's own earlier claims were corrected along the way, every one of them read back out of the handler rather than inferred from the surrounding code.

A bound nobody had asked for

SpellMessages, the table this project's spell work has indexed from the start, turned out to be 13 rows, not an unknown number. This project has given meaning to 11 of them, and not one assigned index falls outside the bound, which would already be good news on its own. But the original reads all 13, 132 reads in total, and two indices, 11 and 12, are still waiting: a named gap, not a fault, because no slice has yet reached what they are for.

And those 13 have a trap of their own. They are elements of an array, so the classification gives them kind 1, not kind 0, and kind 0 is what this project calls a "message". The most read message set in the whole game, 132 reads, is not in the kind a consumer would look for it in. The classification itself is correct; it is the consumer's own assumption about what "message" means that fails.

Eight holes, fourteen strings

Between the named segment's own 50 messages there are eight places where the gap is larger than the neighbouring message's length would explain. The module's own comment names eight identifiers as unused, and eight holes plus eight names is exactly the kind of coincidence that would write itself into a document as an explanation. It was not written: the holes decode into fourteen fully printable strings, not eight. Six are left unaccounted for, and that is recorded as it stands, in originalBugs.md as an anomaly under investigation, not as a bug.

A prediction that held

An earlier slice reasoned, from reading two call sites, that a torch's burn out line and the world clock's own line for a torch burning out on its own should share one index rather than being written twice. The original shares it, and more widely than predicted: one named message, read by four modules. A prediction built from two call sites held across four, and that is this round's warm find alongside the main one.
