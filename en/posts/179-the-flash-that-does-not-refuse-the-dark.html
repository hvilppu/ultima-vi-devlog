The flash that does not refuse the dark

The previous post ended on a promise: the storm rolls the right dice and none of it is visible. It is visible now. The stones fall diagonally, at most six at a time, and a tile flashes under an impact. The interesting part is not the stone but that tile: it is not the same flash as the one under a brawler's fist, and the difference is four measured things, one of which can be seen directly.

One pass per frame, and the sound sets the pace

The original animation is a blocking loop inside the spell's own branch: the player goes nowhere until the storm ends. This view does not block, so it steps the animation one pass at a time. A pass's length is not a chosen number but the length of its own sound, because that MUS_PlaySfx call is the only thing in the original's loop body that waits. The length comes from the sound renderer over that pass's own steps, so a pass that played the noise paces itself differently from one that rattled, and nothing here decides which.

The cap is one pass per frame, and that cap is this view's own choice, not a measurement. With sound off a pass does not wait at all, which is exactly the original's behaviour, and the whole storm would resolve inside a single frame. The cap keeps it visible without changing the number of passes or the dice.

The spell's branch hands the storm over

The new Begin is the shape the original has: the branch decides the halo, the refusal, the message and the sound, and passes the storm on. The old Cast is the same thing run to the end, which is what headless testing needs. Between them sits one test pinning that the same seed gives the identical storm either way: that is the test that would fail if Cast ever grew a roll of its own again.

A stone is a tile at a pixel, not on a square

The composer could draw a tile on a square and the mouse pointer at a pixel. Stones need a tile at a pixel, so that route was built: positioned by its centre, the way the original's own display driver does it. That was worth reading rather than assuming, and the driver's own offset says it outright: 0x588 is eight rows and eight columns of a 176 pixel wide buffer, so a sixteen pixel tile is centred on the point it is given.

The stones are drawn above the frame, which is deliberate rather than careless: the original draws them only after the map window's own draw has already laid the frame into the same buffer, and a stone's own drawing bounds reach into the frame band. It is tested both ways, because a stone drawn under the frame would be invisible exactly where a stone enters the window.

Four differences, and one of them shows

The hail impact flash and the shared hit flash look the same: the same tile. They are not the same thing:

  • It suppresses the shared flash first. Clearing that gate is the only write of nought to it in the whole program, and it sits immediately above the damage routine, whose first act for an actor is to call the shared flash.
  • It is a pixel write and not a cell-stack call, so it inherits none of the refusals the shared flash inherits.
  • Its bounds are tighter than the window's: window squares 0 to 9, where the window itself allows ten. A stone can land two squares from the selection, so the eleventh column is reachable and gets no flash.
  • It plays no sound of its own. The shared flash plays the hit sound; the storm plays its own sound once per pass, with the argument -1 on a pass whose last impact hit an actor.

The second is the one visible on screen: the shared flash refuses a dark square and the hail flash does not. One square, two routes, opposite answers, in one test. And the suppression's consequences are measured rather than merely named: a hail victim gets no tile, no hit sound and no hurt-this-turn bit, and still wakes if it was asleep, because the waking sits outside that gate.

The two-victim case, counted

The original converts the flash coordinates in place, inside the loop, so the second victim on a square is converted from the first victim's pixel rather than from the square. The decompilation's own comment marks it. I wrote a conclusion about it, and the conclusion was wrong.

I wrote into the class's remarks that the twice-converted pixel is almost always out of bounds, so in practice only the first victim is flashed. My own test refuted it on the very first centre it tried. The numbers were counted afterwards, over all 1024 centres: 

centres where a second victim is flashed     100 / 1024  (4-157)
window squares per such centre                 1
a third victim follows                        10 / 100
the second flashed where the first was not    10  (square 10, centres 164-173)
everywhere else no second victim is flashed

That last line is the one no amount of reasoning would have produced: the bounds guard the drawing and not the conversion, so the loop carries on past a victim it refused and the next victim's pixel can land back inside. Recorded as entry 3.39, imitated as the conversion rather than as the conclusion.

And another of the same kind the same evening

The opposite test, the one asserting that no second victim is flashed elsewhere, used 157 as its bound and failed. The reason is that 157 and 173 answer different questions: 157 is where the pair stops being drawn together, and 173 is where the second stops being drawn at all regardless of what happened to the first. Two questions, one number asserted for both. Same class of fault as the previous post, found the same way, by a test.
