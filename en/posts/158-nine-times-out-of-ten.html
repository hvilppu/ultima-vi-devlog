Nine Times Out of Ten

The original's own mouse pointer is on the screen, after three slices. The best thing this measurement produced is not the pointer. It is one shape out of ten that disagrees with the other nine, and that could otherwise have pinned a wrong reading under a green suite.

Three slices: the place, the data, the draw

First the geometry: where the pointer belongs in the map composition. Then the shapes, which are data in the owner's own installation and therefore have to be exported. Last the draw, after the frame, so the pointer sits on top of it.

The geometry slice drew nothing, and my own instruction had assumed it could. When the first slice puts nothing on the screen it is easy to read as a dead end. It was not: the road to the screen was three steps, and reading that took one search.

The one shape out of ten

Every shape declares its own box as four extents, and inside it are runs of pixels. Those give two different readings of a shape's size: read the declared box, or derive the size from how far the runs reach. The original's own arithmetic says the declared box, so that was my brief's answer.

It was still only a citation. The file itself supplied the discriminator: shape 0's declared box is 11 by 15 while its runs reach only 10 by 14. The other nine agree exactly.

So the wrong reading would be right nine times out of ten. That is exactly the shape that survives testing: one test in ten fails, it looks like an edge case, and the bound gets loosened until green comes back. I parsed the exported file myself rather than taking the number out of a report, and the walk closes at 1520 of 1520 bytes.

The crosshair answers outside the map too

The shape choice has five cases and the crosshair is what both branches fall through to. That means the crosshair also appears outside the map window whenever the mouse mode is targeting, not only over the map.

A reimplementation that writes the crosshair as "the map's targeting shape" gets the panel wrong, and nothing about it looks wrong. It was broken on purpose into the inside branch's else, and two tests of 4028 failed while the other four cases passed.

A break that kept every pixel count correct

One break changed no count at all. It was seen only by comparing pixel values, and the reason is that the tests compute their expectations by a different formulation from the code: the draw trims each run at its ends, the test walks every pixel and bounds-tests it individually.

Counting alone would have passed a wrong draw. Had the test been written with the same formulation, it would have been an expensive way of agreeing with itself.

Three errors in my brief, and all three are its own numbers

The height formula dropped the plus one the width kept. One pixel short for every shape. And the interesting part: the reader already had it right, so the brief was the odd one out rather than the authority. The build run caught it by reading the line instead of the brief.

My own table contradicted my own sentence two inches above it. The table lists three rows that reach the crosshair, and the next paragraph said to assert "the two" separately. The difference between two routes and three cases is small, but the instruction read as covering everything.

And the claim that this is the first caller of the direction rule that is not walking: the look command got there first. One search of our own sources would have answered it, and the sentence was written from memory of the walking code.

The consumer rule bit its own author

I had written in the brief that what a shape's four extents mean in pixels is unmeasured. It is measured, in a third consumer I did not look for: the routine that saves the background reads the same four words as geometry, as a width, a height and a top-left corner.

My own sentence did hedge, with "in both routines read here", so the fault is narrower than a false claim. It is naming a gap that one search would have closed, which is precisely the rule this project added after the darkness. Two of three is not a sweep.

Two sources agreed on ten

The number of shapes is what the whole export rests on, and it was measured from two independent sources. The calling code asks for shape 0, shape 1, and a direction plus two, where the direction is one of eight, so it predicts ten. The owner's own file says the same through its bound word: 44, and the directory starts at offset 4 with four-byte entries, so 44 = 4 + 4 * 10.

Neither number is derived from the other. A wrong directory stride or a wrong base would give some other number, and the two agreeing would not be a coincidence.

And that bound admits one index too many

The brief said to measure the bound rather than assume it is the count, and the suspicion came true: because the bound word is the low word of the first directory entry, the test index times four at least 44 admits index 10 and refuses only at 11. Eleven indices for ten shapes.

That is an anomaly and not a bug, because the reachability was measured and closed: the chooser asks only for 0 to 9, and the search found no call site asking for anything else. What was not checked is stated too: this has not been reproduced in DOSBox.

One principle the export wrote more clearly than I did

The double word at the start of the table turned out to be the decompressed length in both real files. The reader exposes it and does not enforce it, because no consumer reads it and enforcing would refuse a file the original accepts.

Expose what you measured, enforce only what the original enforces.

The pointer is doubled over the map, and that is deliberate. Unity's own cursor was left unhidden, because the original's draw for the panel and the icon strip is unmeasured; hiding the cursor before that is built would leave the player with no pointer anywhere outside the map, which is worse than two inside it.
