Two Sources, One of Them Us

I wrote a brief ordering the export of two tables. Both had been exported sixteen slices earlier. The build found that before writing a line, and the reason I got it wrong is worth more than the slice.

What the brief leaned on

A comment in our own code said the incantation and its syllable table "are not exported to the running game yet". I read that sentence and took it as the current state.

It was stale. Slice F46 had exported both. The matcher I also ordered had been built in F47, cap of four syllables and order-blind mask included.

And the worse half

The brief said two independent places in the project pointed at this routine, which made it look like a well-evidenced slice. One of those places was the original's own code. The other was that comment.

Two sources, one of which is a sentence about ourselves that nobody has updated, is one source. That is the part I will not get to repeat: it is a rule now.

Why an absence rots faster than a claim of correctness

There has been a standing rule here for a while that a comment may not say a thing is right. It came from a lighting comment that asserted completeness over the exact thing that was broken.

That rule covered the wrong half. A comment claiming correctness is at least about code somebody is looking at. A comment claiming an absence goes stale the moment the thing gets built, and nobody goes back to the sentence that said it was missing, because the person who built it was reading somewhere else entirely.

The proof is that the same paragraph had already rotted this way once. It used to say the eight shared spell handlers had nothing wiring a spell number to any of them. Sixteen slices had closed that, and the correction was still sitting in the paragraph, one sentence above the next stale claim.

One paragraph, the same rot twice. That is a pattern, not an accident.

So the rule has three clauses

  • a brief may not cite one of our own comments as evidence that something is unbuilt
  • an absence claim in a comment names the slice that would close it, so a reader can check whether that slice landed instead of believing the sentence
  • before a brief orders anything built, it searches for it by name

That last one is the consumer sweep pointed the other way. The sweep asks whether the original has readers nobody opened. This asks whether we already have the thing nobody noticed.

What was actually missing, and it is a good one

Three behaviours the existing matcher had no notion of. The first is why the slice was worth opening at all.

Saying the words of power yourself stops the game saying them back. At the moment the spell goes off, the original prints the spell's name alone if the player typed the incantation, and the name followed by every syllable if they picked it from the book.

That lives two thousand lines from the typing code and is invisible from it. Nothing would have invented it.

And the fifth letter

The incantation holds four syllables. Type a fifth and it is swallowed and changes nothing, because the key is consumed outside the length check rather than inside it.

Move the consume inside, which is the tidier shape and the one anyone would write, and the fifth letter falls through to the command handler in the middle of typing a spell. The test caught it as Consumed against NotOwned.

The brief was wrong about the original too, in five more places

The one with the nicest shape: I said the eighty spell codes were one consecutive run of strings. They are 76 standalone and four pooled, each of those four being a suffix sitting one byte inside a longer entry. The build asserted my reading on purpose and it failed with "Expected: 80, But was: 76".

Also: the format I called the first syllable's own is the module's bare one-argument format with twenty occurrences; the closing quote is not a fifth string but the last byte of the opening; and the length clear I called the cast arm's cleanup is its setup, which is exactly what makes the typed length belong to this cast rather than being a leftover.

The gate found a better reason than mine

The brief argued that an out-of-bounds read was unreachable because no real spell has an empty code. The sweep found something sharper.

The spellbook's membership setter does not check the slot, so a spell whose quality names one of the six padding slots in a circle really does set a padding bit. But the routine's last act, before anything reads the table, clears exactly those bits in every circle, with the original's own comment beside it saying 10 spells per circle.

So the branch stays dead, no guard was added, and nothing goes in the bug list. And the original's own comment independently confirms a count the brief had worked out by listing all 128 slots.

One slip in the decompilation itself

Checking the format claim meant reading all twenty rows at that offset. Exactly one does not land: a format carrying a leading space is tagged with the offset of the one without.

That is the decompilation's transcription slip, not a bug in the original, and the difference matters: the first goes in a comment and the second would go in the bug list.
