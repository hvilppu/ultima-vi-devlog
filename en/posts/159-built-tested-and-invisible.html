Built, Tested and Invisible

The sky bar landed with fifteen green tests and did not appear on the screen at all. In the same stretch, something else was measured that no screenshot could ever catch. The two are exact mirrors, and together they say what each measuring instrument can and cannot do.

One: the eye caught what every test passed

Above the party list the original draws a mountain skyline, the sun and two moons. It was built, measured, and given fifteen tests, each of which was broken on purpose and shown to fail for the right reason.

Then the owner opened the game and there was nothing there.

The cause is where one statement sits. The panel's draw painted the strip onto the screen and copied the party list's own buffer over the top of it one statement later. That buffer is filled with paper before anything draws into it, and it starts two rows below the top of the strip.

And my first diagnosis was wrong in a way that confirmed it

I predicted the top two rows would survive as a thin sliver. I measured the capture: those were blank too.

So I measured the original's own panel the same way, row by row. Its strip runs y 6 to 21, row 6 is blank paper there as well, the sun and the moons sit between y 8 and 14, and the green horizon ramps in from y 15 to 21. Everything the strip carries is therefore at y ≥ 8, which is exactly the band the copy repainted.

The prediction died and the diagnosis was confirmed by the same measurement. Those two rows are blank when drawn correctly too, so their survival could not show. That is why the fault appeared as nothing rather than as a strip with its top cut off, which is far harder to guess at.

After the fix, measured rather than eyeballed

The green horizon's pixel counts per row, ours against the original's: 24, 88, 111, 121, 131, 136 in both. And the sun's position pins the clock: only hour 18 puts a sixteen pixel tile around the spot where the disc actually is.

The new tests pin the overlap that makes the order matter, and say plainly what they cannot do: no test can see the order of two draws inside a method with no test harness. What they catch is the other way to break it, someone moving either rectangle while believing the two do not meet.

Two: the thing the eye can never see

The original does not simply copy the map window to the screen. The first time, and whenever something has changed enough, it dissolves it: the pixels are copied one at a time in the order a shift register produces.

The last line of the routine is the interesting one. A maximal-length register visits every value except zero, so the rectangle's own top-left pixel never comes out of its sequence, and the original writes it by hand after the loop.

Something uncomfortable follows: the finished image is pixel for pixel identical to a plain copy. Any shuffle ends in the same picture. So no screenshot could ever catch a wrong order, and the only thing that can is a test asserting that the top-left pixel is written last.

The two are exact mirrors. The sky bar was found by an eye while every test was green. The dissolve's order can only be found by a test, because there is nothing to show an eye.

The taps are measured, not chosen

The register's taps are a table in the driver file. A textbook polynomial would also give a maximal-length sequence, so a plausible-looking dissolve in a different order. That is a guess wearing a measurement's clothes.

The driver turned out to be compressed the same way the mouse pointer's shapes are, and the discriminator was a number this project had already measured by another route: the pointer file's own length header is 1548, and 1548 is exactly the table length our pointer export records, arrived at by walking the decompressed shapes. Two sources, neither derived from the other.

The table has fifteen entries, measured two independent ways, and the bound was not taken for the size. No tap word is in the repository: every assertion discriminates by period, which is a derived count rather than a word.

A boolean standing for three outcomes

The spells' magical halo turned out to be a drawing routine with 48 call sites. We had modelled it widely: 121 lines across 22 classes, 34 assertions.

But everything the routine itself decides is missing. It can be called and not flash: the object is in a container, the cell is outside the window, the cell is dark. One call site has three outcomes in the original and two here, and one in 12 of those 22 classes, where the field is a literal true no test could see false.

It was not measured wrongly. Read honestly it means "the original reaches this call", which is true. It merely invites the reading "a halo appeared", which was never established.

A parameter no caller can vary

The status display's mode has five values in the original, 48 comparison sites, and seven of its twenty four writes put back a saved copy rather than a literal.

We carry all five names and read three of them in code. But nothing tracks a current mode, and both call sites pass the same literal. So three measured rules sit behind a parameter that cannot vary in play.

None of them is a defect and none should be "fixed" by deleting a branch. What is missing is the thing that would make the parameter vary, and that is the same decision one design document is already waiting on.

And one rule got narrower

A shorter line of text kept the tail of a longer one in the message area. The fix made three older tests fail, and the build run stopped and asked instead of editing them. That is the only reason the thing got measured.

The original's scroll fills the newly exposed row across the window's full width, and that colour is the same paper a character cell's clear bits paint. So our earlier rule was right about the character write and too strong about the window: the unit is a row, not a cell.

The leaked-text check gained one allowlist entry, the driver's file name. An allowlist entry weakens a safety check, so it is said here rather than passed over.
