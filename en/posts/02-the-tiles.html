Rebuilding Ultima VI

Notes from a 1:1 reimplementation in C# and Unity.

The tiles, and a number that made no sense

Two thirds of the artwork is stored in a format whose offsets look wrong, until you work out what they are measured against.

The game has 2048 tiles of 16×16 pixels, and they are spread across three files that have to be read together. TILEINDX.VGA holds 2048 offsets. Decompressed MAPTILES.VGA concatenated with OBJTILES.VGA holds the pixels. MASKTYPE.VGA holds one byte per tile saying how that tile is stored.

Multiply an index entry by 16 and you get a byte offset into the combined pixel stream. The table is monotonic and its last entry lands exactly where the data ends, so that part is easy to confirm. The encoding byte is where it gets interesting. Across all 2048 tiles it only ever takes three values.

ValueTilesMeaning
0439256 bytes, fully opaque
5177256 bytes, index 255 means transparent
101432something else entirely

The first two are easy, and they can be told apart without reading a line of the original code: the 439 tiles marked 0 never contain index 255 anywhere, and all 177 tiles marked 5 contain it at least once. That is not a coincidence, that is a definition.

The run-length tiles

The remaining 1432 tiles are compressed, and they are the majority. Their data begins with a single byte that simply repeats the record's length divided by sixteen, which the index table already told us. After that comes a sequence of records: a 16-bit displacement, a single-byte length, and that many pixel bytes.

So the decoder moves a cursor forward by the displacement, writes some pixels, moves again. Straightforward, except that the displacements are far too big. A tile is sixteen pixels wide and 256 pixels in total, and the most common displacement values are 160 to 175. A single jump would run off the end of two tiles.

I assumed the bit layout was wrong and tried every plausible reading of the record header. None worked. The displacement distribution is what eventually gave it away: 

displacement   count
     0         1624
   165         1577
   164         1555
   166         1480
   167         1422
     1         1384
   163         1294
   168         1206

Two clusters. Small values near zero, and a tight band around 165. If you are drawing a sprite that is, say, eleven pixels wide inside a sixteen pixel tile, the small values are the gaps within a row, and the big ones are the jump from the end of one row to the start of the next. Which means the row width is around 176.

176 is 11×16. It is the width of the game's map window, eleven tiles across. The displacements were never measured against the tile at all. They were written for a cursor walking the screen the tile would eventually be drawn into.

Proving it

A decoder that produces something is not a decoder that is correct, and with graphics the cheapest oracle is your own eyes. I decoded all 2048 tiles, laid them out in a grid, and looked: grass, water, stone walls, wooden floors, barrels, furniture, weapons, and row after row of creatures and people. Unambiguously right.

Then I locked it down so it stays right. The checksum is taken over the decoded palette indices rather than over the finished image file, because an image encoder's compression choices are its own business while the pixel data must not move: 

2048 tiles × 256 indices = 524,288 bytes
SHA-256  07ea4ffe89846acdb8de468474046a3d67f8fde118c81c0d1224f3c961ebcb05

That number is now a test. When the decoder was later rewritten from the prototype into the real C# library, it reproduced the hash byte for byte on the first run, which is exactly the kind of boring confirmation you want from a rewrite.
