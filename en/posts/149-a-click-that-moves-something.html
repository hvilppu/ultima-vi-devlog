A Click That Moves Something

Four slices in a row ended the same way: a rule complete, tested, and called by nobody. This one has a caller. Click inside the map window and the party steps one square toward where you clicked.

And clicking your own square stands still

The rule is a 48 by 48 box around the leader, read as a three by three grid. Eight cells give the eight directions and the middle cell gives none at all, which is how a click on your own square means stand still rather than a direction.

That is measured behaviour and not a convenience. Outside the box the slope decides: the dominant axis gives a cardinal direction when the ratio is at least two to one, and a diagonal otherwise, with the division guarded before it happens rather than after.

And standing still rests on one early return

In the original, the fallback that picks a diagonal sits inside the branch for positions outside the box. So the centre cell's answer leaves by a different door and never reaches it.

Flatten that structure, as anyone tidying would, and the centre cell falls through into the diagonal fallback. Clicking your own square would walk you diagonally, forever, and no test of the eight directions would notice.

The gate asked the wrong question and got a better answer

The brief carried a suspicion: the vertical origin has 22 references where the horizontal has 17, and two values that look like a pair are being used apart somewhere.

They are not. The five extra hits are duplication in the source text: the box branches on x first, so y's two comparisons appear three times where x's appear once, and the diagonal tail tests y in two of x's branches instead of one. Four plus one.

But the sweep the suspicion forced found something else. The origin is not always the leader. Two unbuilt places move it onto a selected object, so that the next direction press means "push that" rather than "walk there".

So the origin is a parameter, and the measured default sits beside the rule as a named constant rather than inside it. The wrong question was worth asking.

Three breaks, on purpose

  • closing the box's upper edge, which is half open: one test failed, expecting 2 and getting 1
  • moving the ratio threshold from two to three: one test failed, expecting 2 and getting 3
  • baking the origin in as a constant: exactly one test failed and every other test in the file still passed, which is what the brief predicted a baked-in constant would do and the reason that test exists at all

Two things are choices here, and they say so

Holding the button does not repeat the step. The original's own guard is true on every poll, so in principle it walks while held. No repeat rate has been read anywhere, and a Unity frame rate would outwalk the keyboard several times over, so the step happens on the press edge and the brief's gap is named rather than filled with a number nobody measured.

And the keyboard wins when both fire in one frame. Not read anywhere either.

There is a third thing underneath both, and it is the one worth remembering: the engine rule answers a direction on every poll only because a flag the original has does not exist here yet. Its condition is therefore always true. That is named in four places in the code, because it stops being true the moment somebody builds the flag.

And then the tile data answered a question twice

The next slice is measured and waiting: the map window's own frame, which has never been drawn here at all. Our build copies the same region of the composition the original copies, and the original fills the outer 8 pixels of it with a border while we leave terrain there.

The frame is eight consecutive tiles around a rectangle, and eight consecutive tiles invite exactly one wrong reading: a row-major three by three with the centre left out. It disagrees with the original in five of eight positions.

Reading the routine settles it. Then the tile data was asked as well, because a reading taken out of one routine is one source.

The lever is that only part of each frame tile ever reaches the screen, and which part depends on which piece of the border the tile is. So score both readings by how many opaque pixels each one puts inside the visible area: 

opaque pixels in the eight tiles      438
visible under this reading            438
visible under the row-major reading   295

This reading wastes nothing. The row-major reading throws 143 pixels away and leaves one whole tile invisible.

And it measured something no reading of the code would have given

47.6 per cent of the border band is transparent. The frame is drawn after the terrain, so the map shows through it: it is not a solid bar. A filled border would have passed every other count above and been visibly wrong.

The near miss worth writing down

The first run of that script counted non-zero bytes as opaque, which is the original's own convention. Every frame tile came back as 256 of 256 and the conclusion was that the data could not tell the two readings apart.

This project's export uses 255 for transparent, not 0. The answer had been there the whole time, hidden behind a number that looked exactly like a real result.
