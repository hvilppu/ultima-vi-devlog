The king under his throne

The conversation engine finally reached the game, and four faults fell out at once. Not one of the 590 tests saw any of them. All four were found by a person looking at the screen.

Talking, in the game

The interpreter had been finished for a long time and nothing in the game used it. Now T talks to whoever is standing next to you, the words appear in a panel under the map window, and typing a keyword works.

The rules went into the engine and only the keyboard and the drawing stayed in Unity. That is not a matter of taste: this project once did the opposite and paid for it with rules that could not be run without an editor.

The hazard the class was written to find

This is the only fault in this post that a test caught, and it caught it because the test was written before I got round to running the game.

Three members of the world refuse to answer until something has said what the window is showing. Until now the only thing that ever set the view was test code. A conversation reached from the game would have refused its first question, and the scripts ask about the screen at 542 sites just to fetch a party member.

The test asserts it in both directions: without a view the question throws, and after starting a conversation it does not.

One place where the shape cannot follow the original

The original stops inside the write: the console fills, it waits for a key right there, and the script resumes when the key comes. Nothing here can stop inside a write, because the conversation is driven a keypress at a time.

And the text layer throws if you write to it while it is waiting. So the surplus is held in a queue and let through as the player presses on. The words arrive in the same order and at the same pauses; only the waiting happens somewhere else.

A test walks a speech through a ten by two panel and checks that every word comes out, in order, with nothing lost. That is the promise the buffering has to keep.

Fault 1: the characters were invisible

I pressed T beside an empty-looking square and got a conversation. The townspeople were in the world and in reach, and nothing drew them.

The cause was not in the drawing but in the export. The original keeps every object and every character in one chain per square, which is why its drawing routine handles both without knowing the difference. This project took the world's objects out of the map blocks and the characters out of the saved game, and my own notes record that the two sets do not overlap at all.

So the merge has to happen when drawing, and that half was missing entirely. I wrote it, and people appeared on the map.

Fault 2: the king under his throne

The next report was one sentence long and landed on something subtle.

A throne is four parts, and the original's own furniture code says which part is which: 

/*sitting on a chair or a throne (throne part 2 = seat)?*/
/*humanoids: 4 frames per facing (1 standing, 0/2 steps, 3 sitting)*/

Part two is the seat. And the part that lands on the seat square is the western half of an object anchored one square east, so it is drawn while the loop is visiting that other square. I drew characters inside the object loop, so the king went down first and the seat was painted over him a moment later.

Characters are their own pass now, between the background objects and the foreground ones. I measured both directions by writing the faulty ordering back in: 

the king's pixels         158
covered by the seat       136  ->  0

One thing I did not settle. In the original a character's place in a square's list follows the order the world's links were built, and the export does not carry that. So the layering of a character against a background object is unresolved. I draw characters afterwards because that is what the one observable fact demands, and I wrote it down as a reading rather than a fact.

Fault 3: the text fell off the screen

A screenshot with the bottom of the conversation hanging out of the game view. This one was arithmetic and embarrassingly small. 

view 1990 x 1290

old:  min(1990/320, 1290/200) = 6   ->  176*6 + 72*6 = 1488
new:  min(1990/176, 1290/248) = 5   ->  176*5 + 72*5 = 1240

The scale was worked out from the original's 320 by 200, while the thing being drawn was the map window with a panel stacked under it. Six times 248 does not fit in 1290.

The sum is in the engine with tests now, and that is the whole lesson: it was two lines long, it was wrong, and nothing in the project could run it, because it lived in a MonoBehaviour. The test that matters is neither of those numbers but the property neither had: whatever is stacked under the window, the result still fits.

Fault 4: "you say:" with nothing after it

The typed line was kept in memory and never drawn, so there was no way to see what you were about to say.

The original echoes a character straight at the cursor with no line buffer, and backspace steps back one, across a line break as well. The point is that this is a different path from a character's speech, and for two reasons: a typed $ would vanish as an escape, and a line that wrapped while being typed would stop to ask for a key in the middle of a word.

I proved the test by taking the echo back out, and the failure message turned up something pleasing: 

"...the thief calls insolently.you say:job"   with the echo
"...the thief calls insolently.you say:"      without

The script prints its own prompt. The original was designed for this, so it was not an addition but a missing half.

What this says about measuring

The last post made much of 172 out of 196, and of an unbuilt world that refuses to answer rather than guessing. That still holds. But this week's four faults say something I had not said out loud.

The number never moved. Not while the characters were invisible, not while the king was under his throne, not while the text fell off the screen, not while you could not see what you were typing. The game was visibly broken four times over and the measure was content throughout.

This is not an argument against measuring. It is an argument for knowing what a measure covers. 172 says how many conversations run from the first word to the last, and it never promised to say anything about what the screen looks like. The problem was that I had been quietly treating it as broader than it is.

Each of the four got a test afterwards that fails without the fix, and three were proved by writing the fault back in. Those tests would not have found the faults. They stop them coming back, which is a different job and still worth doing.
