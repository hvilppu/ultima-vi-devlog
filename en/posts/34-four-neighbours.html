Four neighbours

Characters now walk the original's routes. The previous post had a greedy step standing in for this, and I wrote at the time that it was a divergence. It is gone now.

Four neighbours, not eight

The search never considers a diagonal step. Three squares right and three down is six steps, not three.

This one thing is what makes the routes look like the original's, and it is the first thing that goes wrong: the stand-in I deleted used eight neighbours, because eight is what anybody writes when they write a path search themselves.

Two fronts, and a route that is not the cheapest

The search grows two fronts, one from the character and one from the goal, told apart by the top bit of the cost byte. That is why the cost is seven bits and breaks at 127. And because each front keeps its own cost, the limit is per front and not per route: a wall costing 127 cannot be crossed, a wall costing 60 can.

Then the part that surprised me. The fronts meet the moment one of them looks at a square the other has queued, and the meeting is declared before that square's cost means anything at all.

I wrote a test claiming that a column costing 30 would be walked around, because round is nine steps. The test failed. The route goes straight through: one front had queued the expensive square at 34 while the buckets were still at four, and the other touched it immediately.

A search that compared costs before declaring the meeting would find the cheaper way and would not be this game. I turned the test round and pinned the behaviour as it is.

The raw byte, not a masked one

The path is read back from the meeting square: walk downhill along the costs to the character. The pick is the neighbour whose raw byte is smallest.

I wrote it more carefully than that: I masked the cost to seven bits and filtered by front. That is meant to be the same thing, stated more clearly.

It is not. The front bit is the top bit of the same byte, so a square on the character's front always compares smaller than one on the goal's. The raw comparison keeps the walk on the right front without anyone having to say so.

The masked version walks the wrong way. And when I tried it deliberately later, it hung the loop forever and locked up the test process. I added a bound to both walk loops that the original does not have, because a hung game is worse than a wrong route.

The resistance map, and bridges

The cost is one byte per square: the terrain's movement cost plus one, or blocked. Objects go on top of that, and one of the object rules turns a blocked square into a walkable one: 

the square is blocked
the terrain is WET
the object's tile is not impassable
  -> the square opens at the object's own cost

That is a plank over water. Four surface windows hold 21 bridge squares.

And because the bridge rule asks whether the square is blocked right now, the rules are not commutative: objects have to be walked in chain order. Walking square by square would give a different map wherever two objects meet, and it would look perfectly sensible.

One thing I did not expect: characters add no resistance. The original tells characters from objects by number, and only the object branch adds a cost. So a route is planned straight through somebody standing in the way.

Three searches a turn

At noon 36 characters set off. Three of them get a route that turn, because the search is capped at three and there are eight path slots.

That is visible behaviour, not throttling: a town sets off in a trickle rather than all at once. Measured in the Avatar's window, the budget is spent as the series 3, 1, 0, 0, 0, because of the 36 sent somewhere only four stand inside that window.

The best check on the whole search

I asked for every walkable square in the Avatar's window in turn, and walked each route found out step by step: 

walkable goals           1121
route found               514
no route                  607
ended in the wrong place    0
longest route              38 steps

Nought ending in the wrong place out of 514. And the 607 without a route are not the budget's fault: the same sweep with 4096 nodes instead of 256 finds exactly the same 514, so unreachability is walls.

What is left

Five things, all of them recorded. Teleporting, which is what the original does with a character outside the window, unless it is within five squares of the player. Heading for the window's edge. Recovering when a route being followed gets blocked. Seeking furniture, which is how a sleeper finds a bed. And movement points.
