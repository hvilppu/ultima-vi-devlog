A test that could not fail

Three of the seven spells left in the missile switch, neither of which creates or destroys a creature: lightning, chain bolt and dispel field. Two of them now actually act when cast from the spellbook. But the interesting part is not what works. It is that the first attempt did not, and no test noticed.

Two simple cases and one that stays unwired

Lightning is the simplest case in the whole switch: one 1d30 roll, no contest, no immunity check, exactly the energy wind's own effect on a single target. Chain bolt is a first hit followed by up to six hops, each one from the current victim to the next, never from the caster. Both are now wired, bringing the missile switch's built count to 18 of 23, five left.

Dispel field is the third, and it stays unwired on purpose. Its own routine exists and is tested: it walks the selected square's objects and deletes the first of four field types. But it reads the square, not a resolved target object, and the missile switch has no level, tile bank or base tile table on hand to give it. The gap is now named by its own test rather than papered over. The same slice also closed an old open question: why dispel field is the one object-demanding spell in the missile switch's 23 that is allowed to pass through aimed at an actor. The reason is now measured, not just named: it never reads the selected object at all.

The centre that was not the victim

Chain bolt's next target is picked from a window nine squares wide, centred on something. The brief said only "the centre", and the first implementation read that as the current victim's own square, because the next filter measures distance from exactly that victim. It was the only reading the wording allowed.

The original uses two different origins in the same test: the window is the party's own screen centre, the distance is the current victim. The decompilation's own comment for the routine, "within 5 tiles of the screen center", is the sentence that settled it. The consequence is not a detail: the chain can never leave the nine by nine box around the party, however far it hops. A victim at the edge of that box can see targets within five squares of itself that sit outside the box, and none of them qualifies.

A symmetry that could not fail

The second defect is more interesting, because it is not a reading mistake but a flaw in the test itself. When two candidates are exactly as far from the current victim, a tie is broken by a coin flip. The brief said the flip keeps the incumbent one way and takes the new candidate the other, and asked for both directions to be asserted in the test.

That is a symmetry assertion, and a symmetry assertion passes whichever way the code has it. A test that claims "one direction or the other works" can never tell you which direction is correct, because both directions are one direction or the other. The first implementation copied its nearest relative, the target picking routine that otherwise shares chain bolt's whole window walk, and that routine takes the new candidate on one. Chain bolt does the opposite: nought takes the new candidate, one keeps the incumbent. One idiom, two handlers, the direction inverted by one character of source in the original, and a test that asserted both directions never noticed it was pointed the wrong way.

The fix is not complicated: pin the direction with a named generator seed, to two named answers, not two outcomes that would both have been accepted. The point worth keeping is that neither defect failed a single test or looked wrong. Both were the kind of thing that could have carried straight through to the next slice unnoticed, because neither was the original's own mistake. Both were this project's, born from a brief's ambivalent wording, so both are recorded in the plan and not in originalBugs.md.
