Where a die sits in the stream

Conjure and summon were the last spell family still answering with a named refusal. The blocker was written down precisely: the original creates the creature before the eight placement draws, and the generator could not be wired in without reordering those draws. The order is fixed now and both spells put a real creature on the map. Reading the handler for it turned up a second ordering problem nobody had named.

Why one die's position is a rule and not a detail

This game has one random stream. Everything reads it: combat, schedules, spells, eggs. If a routine takes one draw in the wrong order, it does not merely get its own answer wrong; it shifts the whole rest of the stream, and the next hour's fights roll different numbers than the original's would from the same seed.

So "wire in the generator" was not a small job. The generator draws for itself, for statistics and possessions, and those draws belong between the creature roll and the placement draws.

And a second die was already in the wrong place, waiting for a caller

Reading the handler through turned this up: the summoned daemon's own resistance test is inside the loop, after a successful placement, and it draws a 1d30 of its own.

Our implementation took the answer as a ready made boolean from its caller. There was no caller, so nothing was broken, but the signature would have put that die wherever the caller rolled it. It is the kind of defect that only appears when something finally calls you, and by then nobody is looking here.

Four resistance formulas, not three

Settling the daemon's test meant counting the resistance formulas, and there are four: 

spell.c:490  ((strength/2)  + 30 - strength) / 2 > 1d30
spell.c:496  ((strength/2)  + 30 - intellect)/ 2 > 1d30
spell.c:502  ((dexterity/2) + 30 - intellect)/ 2 > 1d30

combat.c:248  (intellect/2) + 15 - intellect      > 1d30

The first three share a shape; the fourth does not. This project's two classes model exactly those two shapes, and the daemon's test uses the fourth. Neither needed changing, which is knowledge only once checked: without the check it would have been an assumption.

Three endings, and one of them must not print the success row

The wiring returns three different outcomes, because the original has three endings: the creature lands and answers to you, the daemon lands and turns on you, or all eight attempts fail and the creature is deleted.

The middle one is interesting. The original prints its own literal line there, not an exported row, and this project does not carry the original's words. The easy shortcut would be to print the friendly success row, which is exported and to hand. That would have told the player a hostile daemon answers to them, which is the one wrong answer available. So the line is this project's own sentence, and the outcome has a code of its own that says why.

And the deletion is a rule, not a tidy up

When all eight attempts fail, the original deletes the creature. That is not memory management but the rule: the creature was made, it did not fit anywhere, and it stops existing. The view puts the slot back and takes the creature out of the index, which is the same split every other caller of that pair keeps.

The leaked-text check caught a spell name in a test

In the same pass: I wrote a family census that counted outcomes by a name prefix. The prefix is six characters and it is also one of the original's spell names. The leaked-text check caught it in a test file, where it would otherwise have sat unnoticed. The census uses nameof now, with no literal at all.
