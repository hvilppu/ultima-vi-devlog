The square nobody asked

In the original, every step asks a square two things: can you stand on the ground, and who is already standing there. This project had four separate movement paths and all four asked only the first. Now all four ask both, and a creature on a square finally stops you. Around it a lot more landed: a townsperson who arrives now sleeps in the bed instead of lying on nothing, a dock and a ship's deck can be walked onto, a horse opcode answers instead of throwing an exception, and a creature holding a spell weapon actually casts it. And along the way the project swept its own claims twice, with a bigger sample each time, and found more stale sentences before anyone could build on top of them.

The square nobody asked

The Avatar's own step asked a square just one class, and that class takes a tile bank, an object index and the base tiles, and no actor at all. Its own comment said plainly that walls, water and furniture stop the step, which is accurate about what it does and silent about what it does not. The original blocks an actor through that actor's own tile's impassable flag rather than through its being an actor, which is why a reader looking for an actor test finds none.

The first count against the owner's own export: of 200 live characters, 185 stand on an impassable tile and would block a walker in the original, 3 stand on a passable one and would not block even there, and 12 have no usable tile. The count was wrong, and the correction made the defect bigger rather than smaller. The real rule: an actor blocks because it is an actor, whatever its own tile says, unless it is one of five small critter types and a different type than the mover itself. The tile test only decides which route reaches that rule. Of the 200, only one, a mouse, is one of the five critter types; the other exception is slot zero, an empty placeholder at the origin. 198 of 200 block. Proved by breaking it: a dog sits on a passable tile and is not a critter, and still blocks; dropping the mouse from the critter list fails exactly the test that names it.

The Avatar's own step now asks both. The same question was then measured for the other three movement paths, the party's follow, an NPC's errand step, and a vehicle's move, and all three asked the same one class and none of them asked it about an actor either. The party's follow and an errand step now share one new adapter, which carries the same own-tile arithmetic that used to sit inside the view, and all four call sites go through it instead of repeating it. The vehicle path was deliberately left unwired: a ship, a skiff and a balloon ask about wet tiles and walls, the actor-collision class carries only the walker's impassable flag, and wiring it in would answer the wrong question with the wrong flag. One smaller gap was named rather than patched: an errand NPC is not blocked by an able, non-leader party member on an otherwise impassable tile, because the check never asks whether the mover is player controlled.

And one gap is already waiting for the next slice. The original derives flying, swimming and being ethereal from the creature's own monster class, never from a vehicle, and here the mover's kind still comes from the vehicle alone. The mover is now a set of five flags, matching the original's five conditions exactly, rather than one name to choose from, but nothing calls it through a creature yet. Fourteen of the 200 live characters fly, every one of them a winged gargoyle, and they still walk by the walker's own rule.

Arriving, and now doing something

The original's own turn frame ends every schedule mode with no arm of its own by spending five points as a default, and only then does a backstop take ten from an actor whose points never moved at all. Here there was no default, so every scheduled behaviour fell through to the backstop and paid double. Measured against the owner's own schedules.bin: 563 entries, 18 behaviours, of which 328 are postures with no turn arm even in the original, 214 have a real rule, and 21 sit elsewhere. The default five, the halving for a mounted actor, and the shared wander and loiter rules are all built now.

328 postures got their first visible side this slice. A townsperson told to sleep lies in the bed, one told to sit sits down, one told to eat turns to face its plate, one told to play the lute plays it. The same code reuses the square's own object list, the asleep flag and the shape change exactly as the camp's own unconscious guard already does.

The build run corrected my own table three times from text I had read line by line. The asleep flag is set before the area test fires, so out of area the flag stays set even though the bed search is skipped. The prefix that clears the asleep flag and restores the shape runs before every action's own area test, so it fires for wandering, loitering and farming too, not only the six postures. And standing and guarding carry no area test at all. I had generalised "every arm" from the four that actually do it.

Sitting on the throne

The seat search answered no to every throne, and the reason was in the comment: our own covering square's part offset might not match the original's. That question had already been answered in a different brief weeks earlier, with the same table, and the comment never caught up. The posture slice read the comment, believed it, and wrote a sibling chair-and-throne search rather than closing the first one. Counted: originalBugs.md 2.10 measured that 7 of the 81 sitting schedule entries name a throne. The search was rewritten to read the covering parts instead of a plain square list, the old sibling search was deleted, and a rule came out that neither brief had carried: a throne always seats the NPC facing south regardless of its own stored frame, while a chair uses its own frame. Seven thrones and 74 chairs, none left unresolved.

Onto the dock, into the saddle, into the spell

The ground's verdict is a starting value in the original, not a decision, and an object can overturn it. The walker's collision code returned false on impassable ground before it looked at any object at all, so a dock, a pier and a ship's deck were all unwalkable, and this is exactly the path the Avatar's own step, the party's follow and every NPC errand step go through. The rule was missing as a shape, not as one condition, which is why the brief asked for the whole shape to be rebuilt rather than for one more early exit: the class could only ever block or ignore, never permit, even though the original has two branches that do permit. I attacked this exact claim against my own worst case before it touched the game's most used rule, because it was the strongest claim of the session and the one with the most to lose if wrong. It held, and the attack found something stronger still: the class cannot express a permit at all, structurally.

Measured against the owner's own export: nine schedule destinations sit on impassable ground, all ten of those squares hold exactly one object, and it is one object type whose four frames all carry the bridge flag. More widely, 42 of 2048 tiles carry it, including the ship's own hull frames, the skiff and the raft. One existing number moved because of the fix, and it is a result rather than an input: the vehicle path's walkable square count rose from 293629 to 293631, because two squares have a bridge followed by a blocker in the same chain, and chain order decides which one wins.

Three smaller things landed at the same time. Three conversation scripts call a horse opcode that used to throw an exception nobody caught; it now moves the NPC to the view centre, writes the mounted horse shape from the NPC's own facing, and creates a fresh rear half one step behind, since no horse object exists anywhere in this path. No guards: the opcode calls the mount routine directly rather than the use command, so all five of the use command's own refusals are simply not asked. And a creature that chooses a spellbook or a charged object as its weapon now casts instead of doing nothing: the rule already existed and was already tested, only the call was missing. 33 of 200 live characters belong to a spell-carrying class, fourteen of them the same winged gargoyles the flying gap names.

A name and a face, and where both of them actually come from

The look command already had the case for drawing a face, but the branch was an empty break, with a comment explaining why: the view's own one-line status log has nowhere to put a portrait. That was true when it was written. The conversation window has its own text panel now, so the branch was filled in: looking draws the face, waits for a key where neither of the conversation's own call sites waits, and the head resolution class gets what was claimed to be its first caller ever.

Claimed, because that part was wrong. The brief said this would be the head resolution's first caller ever, and that the project had recorded twice that it had none. It already had one: the search found the calling line, and the result was dismissed as a comment because most of the surrounding lines genuinely were comments. Same search, same line, wrong reading. Withdrawn.

The same search left behind a different, real finding. The object world and the character table are disjoint id spaces, and the look path undid the actor offset early, so a plain object's raw world index shared a number space with the character numbers. The first measurement read this backwards: it claimed eighteen conversations show somebody else's belongings. They do not. The carried-by call keys on an object's owner field, and an owner below 256 IS a character number, guaranteed by a routine in the original's own writer that fixes it at save time. Those eighteen wear their own equipment correctly. What was actually broken: the look path's own offset collision meant that looking at an object whose raw index happened to equal a real character's number read that character's pack. And character 7 got a generic face instead of its own, because the object sitting at index 7 is a wisp type. Both are fixed by one shared resolution in the engine, used by both drawing methods and the look path now, so the two cannot drift apart again.

The conversation window draws the name too, now, read the same way the original reads it: from the party slot for a party member, from the script's own header for everyone else, never from a table that would have been easy to assume. Two of the original's own oddities survive unfixed, and both are already in originalBugs.md: character zero's name comes from a different number than its face, and the met-or-not test reads "the flag byte is zero" when it means "the met bit is clear," because the negation binds before the mask.

A thousand fights without exceptions

M14's own acceptance test is a fight between a rat and a skeleton that ends like the original's, plus a suite that runs a thousand simulated fights without exceptions. Both needed something that was missing: the turn logic lived in the view, 123 lines of Unity dependency that turned out to be exactly one redraw flag. The turn moved into the engine, and the suite grew by exactly five tests and not one elsewhere, which is what a move that changes nothing else should do.

The rat and the skeleton were measured against the owner's own monster table rather than by simulating our own rules against themselves. The skeleton wins: two and a half times the hit points, higher dexterity, an attack with strength the rat does not have. The rat cannot flee: flee needs intelligence of at least five and the rat's is four. And the rat's poison does do something, even though the first guess was that it would do nothing against an immune skeleton: the original's own poison gate tests only the attacker's poisonous trait and a one-in-four roll, never the defender's immunity. So an immune creature can be marked poisoned and simply take no damage from it. The thousand fights: 86 milliseconds, 25 rounds at the longest, 5.7 on average, and two known defects were caught along the way, a wander response with no performance behind it and a turn closing before the move it was charging for, both fixed now.

Claims that had gone stale

The last post listed five mistakes from one day. This slice found more, through two systematic sweeps rather than by chance.

The first swept one file's own absence claims about the object world: 75 opened, 6 stale, all in the same shape, true when written and overtaken once something got built. The second swept the whole Assets/Scripts tree for the same kind of absence sentence, unfiltered and untruncated: 265 claims opened across 110 files, nine stale. One of them turned out to be copied into a second file, and both copies were corrected together.

And two single claims were withdrawn by reading rather than by a sweep. The head resolution's "first caller ever" was wrong, with the search result sitting in my own output and dismissed as a comment too quickly. The rat's poison "does nothing" was wrong, inferred from a trait table rather than read from the handler that actually gates it. Both are the same shape: not a missing search, but a search's own answer thrown away, or a trait table taken in place of the handler. Claims do not only go wrong by going stale; some are wrong from the moment they are written, and the only way to tell the two apart is to open the line the claim is about.
