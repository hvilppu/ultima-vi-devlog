Rebuilding Ultima VI

Notes from a 1:1 reimplementation in C# and Unity, one file format at a time.

This is a devlog for a personal project: taking a legally owned copy of Ultima VI: The False Prophet apart, understanding every file on its disks, and rebuilding the game as a native C# program that behaves exactly like the original. No remaster, no quality-of-life changes. The same screen, the same rules, the same bugs.

The posts below are about the engineering, in the order the work happened. There are no dates because the only thing worth tracking is what actually runs.

Where it stands

The bars are derived from the plan rather than written by hand, because a hand kept board drifts from what it claims to read. Two numbers, measuring different things: 13 of 24 milestones finished, and inside them 158 of 184 work items. The second is the one that moves while a milestone is still in flight.

Every bar opens to show the plan entries it counted. A bar on its own is a claim with nothing behind it, and listing the entries is what makes the count checkable. Opening them up found two faults in the plan straight away that the total alone had hidden.

  1. 01 What is actually on the disks

    Fifty-odd data files, no documentation, and one compression scheme standing between me and all of it.

  2. 02 The tiles, and a number that made no sense

    Two thirds of the artwork is stored in a format whose offsets look wrong, until you realise what they are measured against.

  3. 03 Assembling Britannia

    A million tiles squeezed into 32 KB, and an ordering mistake that looks perfectly plausible until you render it.

  4. 04 Sixteen thousand objects and one suspicious bit

    Finding the flag that separates things lying on the ground from things inside a chest, by elimination.

  5. 05 Someone already did the hard part

    A decompilation of the original exists. It removed the project's biggest risk and immediately caught a bug of my own.

  6. 06 The first picture

    Five milestones of invisible work finally show up on screen, specifically by not using the game engine's tilemap at all.

  7. 07 The invisible sea

    The renderer drew a blank white square. A third of the world turns out to have no artwork at all, and the test that should have caught it was asking the wrong question.

  8. 08 The gap in the wall

    A hole appeared beside every doorway. Some objects cover two squares, and only half of each was being drawn.

  9. 09 What the characters say

    Conversations are bytecode. The reader appeared to work on the first try, and it was wrong in a way only counting could show.

  10. 10 The king's day

    The last unverified file format. You recognise the right bit layout by the fact that nobody ends up in the sea.

  11. 11 The written word

    Books and signs. The same percent sign means two different things depending on which route the text takes.

  12. 12 The words of magic

    The spells are compiled into the program. What gave the table away was a feature that started life as headroom for spells never added.

  13. 13 Experience that leads nowhere

    Experience never raises a level at all. And halfway through I caught myself having written a claim in the documentation that was false.

  14. 14 The rule I did not follow

    The plan said from the start to keep the game logic independent of Unity. The bill only arrived when the tests could not be run.

  15. 15 The machine that talks

    The conversation interpreter runs all 199 scripts. The interesting part was three rules that are wrong and must not be corrected.

  16. 16 Only the first one answers

    Keyword matching. The four letter truncation is a story rather than a rule, and the corpus test found three things, the worst of them mine.

  17. 17 The part that asks

    Conversations work end to end for 198 characters. One is hard of hearing, and one of my tests could not fail.

  18. 18 199 out of 199

    The last missing instruction, a character number that read as one, and a second test of mine that distinguished nothing.

  19. 19 Four letters, in the wrong place

    The conversation engine is finished. The story people tell about this parser is true, but about a different instruction.

  20. 20 The font that was not there

    The runic font is the upper half of the same font. A class of mine was in the wrong assembly, and the flags turned up an original fault.

  21. 21 The number that was missing

    I set out to put characters on the map and found the exported objects had no number. Two measurements landed on the same two characters.

  22. 22 Nine squares, not eleven

    Characters on the map. Being on screen is a more particular rule than I expected, and one part of it is not a filter.

  23. 23 A plausible no

    172 conversations out of 196 get through against the real world. The number is measurable only because the unbuilt world refuses to answer.

  24. 24 The test that protected the bug

    Six refuters against my own claims, and not one survived intact. The worst finding was a test I had just written to protect a bug.

  25. 25 The king under his throne

    Conversation reached the game and four faults fell out. None of the 590 tests saw any of them, and the measure never moved.

  26. 26 The check that confirmed itself

    The party follows and people can be recruited. And a check written to stop false reports of work being done gave me one.

  27. 27 The number that would not move

    The measure sat at 172 through five real fixes. It is 190 now, and why it moved is more interesting than the new number.

  28. 28 The number that was a position

    Object numbers are not on disk, only a position in a list. Resolving it wrongly would look like real data, so the wrong reading was measured and failed in a test.

  29. 29 The discriminator that discriminated wrongly

    My first proof of the weight table's position was wrong: a flag table scored better than the weight table. The second discriminator worked, and the measure moved by one, not two.

  30. 30 The decision before the act

    M12's first slice is the decision whether an object can be taken. The order of the checks is part of the rule, and the four objects that are too heavy are eggs.

  31. 31 The bug that had not happened yet

    The object world can change now. But the whole reason was a bug nothing had committed yet, because nothing had moved yet.

  32. 32 Two that are not mirrors

    Things can be picked up now. And giving and taking are not one machine pointed two ways, though I said they were.

  33. 33 The town woke up

    At twelve o'clock 36 characters set off. The rule had been read months ago, and the handler still corrected my implementation in four places.

  34. 34 Four neighbours

    Characters walk the original's routes. Four neighbours and not eight, and a route that is not the cheapest, because the fronts meet too early.

  35. 35 Teleporting Is the Default

    The docs claimed the search came first and teleporting was the fallback. That was backwards, and the difference is measured at four characters.

  36. 36 A Search Nobody Ever Calls

    Heading for the window's edge is now built. Seeking furniture is measured and left unbuilt, because no shipped data ever starts it.

  37. 37 A Blocked Step Is Now a Three Hour Thing

    Movement points are in the engine now. Keeping the hourly round meant the three-try blocking escalation now plays out hour by hour rather than step by step.

  38. 38 Looking in the Wrong File for Years

    The pixel encoding of .SHP frames is solved. The module map pointed at the wrong file for years: drawing happens in a loadable graphics driver, not the game program.

  39. 39 Points Never Pause the Player

    The leader's and the party's own movement-point charge is in the engine. The points are real, but a key press always works the same way, the owner's own choice.

  40. 40 Four Vehicles and One of Our Own Bugs

    A ship, a skiff, a raft and an inflated balloon now sail in the engine. Found along the way: our own bug, a walker who could not wade into the shallow when it should.

  41. 41 The Gate Always Goes Where the Moon Is

    Moongates work in the engine. Found along the way: STARPOS.DAT is the intro film's own starfield and has nothing to do with the game at all.

  42. 42 Karma Wraps Before It Stops

    The Gargoyle language flag and karma are in the engine. Karma's raising opcode wraps as a byte before it clamps, exactly like the original, and the game state block that the same bug turned up twice is finally the right length.

  43. 43 The Shrine Speaks by Quality

    The main quest is fully measured: it is objects and flags, not a state machine. The quest flag and talking to shrines by quality are in the engine; the rest waits on a missing use command.

  44. 44 One Meal Is Enough for Eight

    M17 is done. There is no hunger clock, proved five ways, but a bard's own melody drives camp mealtime: without one, a single meal heals the whole party.

  45. 45 That Byte Must Not Be Computed

    Writing a save is underway. One coordinate byte is uninitialized stack with no formula for it, and for the same reason so is a written name, "Avatar" after its own NUL.

  46. 46 Not Even the Original Gets the Same Byte Twice

    The save folder stayed empty because a DOSBox overlay mount was quietly sending it somewhere else entirely. The real save turned up, and a second one from the same data proved the original itself never reproduces its own leftover byte either.

  47. 47 A Third Project Because Two Must Not Know Each Other

    The live world and the save file's own byte layout must not reference each other, so meeting them needed a third, small project. An object's area comes from its root, not from the object itself.

  48. 48 Two Fields the Export Has Never Known

    The same bridge for characters: the live character table turns into OBJLIST's own bytes. Along the way, two fields this project's own export has never carried needed measuring first.

  49. 49 Zero Exceptions Out of Sixty Nine

    The load bridge built, and run for the first time against a real, played save: every one of the 69 blocks kept its exact record count.

  50. 50 The Simplest Case Was Only One Of Eight

    U6's "use" verb measured in full: 85 types, not hundreds. Of the eight simplest looking handlers, seven waited on a missing subsystem.

  51. 51 Eighty Five Types, Fifteen Rules

    The dispatch table mostly filled in: fifteen measured rules, eight types with no engine side at all, and one rule that does not fit the interface.

  52. 52 Fifty Monster Classes Out Of Sixty Four

    M14's first slice: the Attack command and combat.c's own tables, measured and built. The decompilation's addresses ten bytes off, and granting experience behind one bit rather than the whole alignment value.

  53. 53 A Sequence Of Twelve Actions

    M14's second slice: the turn order, measured and built. Three wrong selection rules give exactly the same totals as the right one, so the test pins the order actors act in rather than a total.

  54. 54 Two Handlers Needed Five Supporting Pieces

    The first piece of M14's third slice: entering combat, leaving it and a charm ending. A known rule ran into five supporting pieces missing from the character record.

  55. 55 A Pixel at a Time, Not a Tile

    Combat's line of fire measured: the original walks a shot in pixels, not tiles, and a tile stepping version would be systematically more permissive, not merely different.

  56. 56 A Variable Nothing Reads

    Combat's own geometry measured: the party's and the enemies' gravity centres, a front member and four flank slots. One of the six selected variables is written every turn, and nothing in the whole decompilation ever reads it.

  57. 57 A Coin That Favours the Last One

    Target and weapon choice, and the basic AI that attacks with them. The coin that breaks a tie favours the last candidate half the time no matter how many are tied, and an NPC must not cast, even though the gate for it is built.

  58. 58 Thirty-Six of Forty-Five

    What a ranged shot leaves behind, the last half of M14's slice A. A thrown weapon is only lost on nine of forty-five wet tiles, the deep ones, and a flask of oil reads the same byte from a different bit.

  59. 59 Three of Twelve

    Four positioning modes, M14's third slice. Assault, flank and berserk each pick a target by a different rule and agree in exactly three of twelve cells, and berserk pays twice on every path.

  60. 60 Three of Sixteen

  61. 61 870 of 900

  62. 62 61 of 80

  63. 63 Two Helpers, Five Quiet Differences

  64. 64 825 of 900

  65. 65 The Guard the Next Line Steps Over

  66. 66 A Light Level That Is Not a Level

  67. 67 The Spell That Cannot Be Resisted

  68. 68 A Clock With a Reason to Tick

  69. 69 A Grip That Lets Go on Its Own Turn

  70. 70 The Armour Nobody Added Up

  71. 71 Gold For One Sentence

  72. 72 The Party Hits Back

    The player's own Attack command, M14's slice F2. The party can hit back and a spawned creature can die; the handler put the ammunition gate ahead of the range gate, and the glass sword always hits without a roll.

  73. 73 Eight Attempts At A Square, Not A Creature

    Creating an actor, the clone, and the slime's own division, M14's slice F3. The object store's own sister primitive for the actor side; a dividing slime's eight attempts look for a passable square, not a chance to create a creature, which is the easiest detail to get backwards.

  74. 74 The Local Bit That Belongs To The Head, Not The Wing

    The creature generator, M14's slice F4. The gazer's own insects now close in all three real call sites, and a dragon's own head carries the local bit a future purge will look for while its four wing and body tiles must not carry it.

  75. 75 Three Bytes That Were Not Zero

    What a creature is carrying, M14's slice F5. Two new export tables turned up by searching, ten index entries disagreed with the transcription and two bytes in the blob were not zero where they were supposed to be; both reported, neither guessed fixed.

  76. 76 An Egg That Hatches Only Once

    The eggs hatch, M14's slice F6. A failed roll still spends the egg, a mode leaking from the first embryo and an amulet test that always asks the Avatar are recorded as faults, and the F9 development key now names this slice as what it stood in for.

  77. 77 A Boat That Never Paid For Its Own Step

    A vehicle's own movement cost, M16's slice A. The previous slice left a boarded vehicle's step charging nothing, and the clock stopped running on a lone trip; the ship, the skiff, the raft and the balloon now pay the real, measured cost, and the regression is pinned by a test that failed before the fix.

  78. 78 Four Seconds Behind One Step

    This project's own performance defect, found by the owner in play rather than by any test. A helper that rebuilt the object world's three lookup dictionaries from scratch on every mutation became the most expensive thing in the game once eggs started hatching mid walk; it is incremental now, about eighty-eight times faster by the same measurement that found the problem.

  79. 79 A Pool That Never Gave A Slot Back

    M14's slice F9, and another fault of this project's own: the eggs hatch but nothing ever freed a temporary actor's slot or let a far egg try again. Read straight out of OBJ_PurgeFarLocals, with an asymmetric forty tile window centred on the loaded area rather than the party, and run before the hatch sweep rather than after.

  80. 80 Two Questions No Test Ever Asked

    2051 passing tests saw neither of the last two posts' own defects, because neither was a wrong answer: one is a cost, counted in records rather than timed, and the other is an invariant over a run, measured false in its expected shape and fixed to the true one. Both tests are shown failing against the commit just before their own fix.

  81. 81 An Egg Competing With The Whole Continent

    The new harness's first catch: egg hatching swept the whole level every time rather than the party's own 40 by 40 area, filling all 32 temporary slots on every single sweep. Fixed to bound the sweep to the area, everything else left untouched, and measured: the busiest real area holds 17 eggs, never 32.

  82. 82 The Bit That Would Have Looked Like Paralysis

    One status bit connected to three gaps three earlier slices had already measured and left waiting: five sight rules in target selection, clearing invisibility on death, and the reveal spell's own ring move. The one way to get it wrong: the invisible bit belongs on the object's own status byte, not the character's, whose same bit value is paralysed.

  83. 83 The Ship That Refused Too Soon

    The skiff and raft redirect, a ship becoming a raft, and breakable objects: three of the four branches applying a hit had named as refusals. Along the way, the ship's own check turned out to sit in the wrong place: it refused every hit, not only the lethal one, for a couple of hundred posts without anyone noticing.

  84. 84 The Serpent That Stacks Itself

    The silver serpent's own segment chain and the tangle vine pod's four vines, both named refusals from the eggs slice. The serpent's four offsets are relative to the head, never the segment before it, so a longer serpent stacks itself on the same four squares rather than walking outward.

  85. 85 The Book That Cannot Draw Itself Yet

    The spellbook's own model: which spells are known, which is selected, which page is shown and how many times a line can be cast, all in the engine with nothing drawn. The "previous circle" button was measured broken and reproduced as such: it jumps two circles back instead of one when the page starts partway into its own circle.

  86. 86 The Button That Never Selects

    The spellbook's navigation and its own thin drawing: four direction keys, four clickable buttons, and a finding that a button and a key disagree about whether the selection changes. The previous circle button's pinned fault turned out to reach real navigation, not only the number it displays.

  87. 87 The Second Slot That Never Says No

    What puts a spellbook into a party member's hand: two previous slices left the question open, this one closes it. The original's own second book slot never guards itself against a third equipped book, and that is reproduced exactly as measured.

  88. 88 The Third Dormant Trap

    The spell table had been read out of the program and tested months ago, and it had never once been carried into the running game. The third time in the same session that correct, tested, dormant code turned into a defect the moment something started calling it.

  89. 89 The Number That Was Supposed To Be A Name

    Both a spell's own name and its circle's name had already been read out of the program months ago; the gap was that nobody had carried them into the running game, the same shape as the previous slice's own reagents. A fixed record cannot hold a name that changes length, which opened a second fault a plain truncation check cannot catch.

  90. 90 The Mark That Edited Its Own List

    Picking up an object gained target selection, stealing and karma. Marking a stolen container's whole content walked the very list it was editing, and a three level test caught it before anybody got the chance to play into it.

  91. 91 The Slot That Never Asks the Type

    Readying was the missing link in the spellbook's whole chain. Which slot an object occupies is settled by its tile, never its type, through eight tests run in a fixed order, and the one handed table's own thirty three tiles were located in the owner's own game file rather than trusted from the decompilation's comment.

  92. 92 Nine Gates Before the First Spell

    The capstone of seven measuring slices and three building slices: selecting a line in the spellbook now actually casts. Nine gates in exactly the measured order, six of them a refusal, each tested on its own to prove nothing is ever spent for nothing.
